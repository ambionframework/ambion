Who wakes when you open it

An arrival is a message with no words in it. Every other message says what it wants; an arrival says only that somebody is here, so a product answering one is guessing at the request — and three products guessing hands you three briefings you never asked for. But a product whose job is to meet you at the door should wake.

So the decision belongs to the seat, not to the room. A seat's attention is one widening scale — named hears only what is addressed to it, broadcast also hears anything said, presence also wakes when somebody arrives or leaves. The session routes a presence message like any other; each seat says whether it is wide enough to be woken. Here the task list is seated attentive and the other two are not.

What this run dropped

Two things came out of the visit before this run, and the run is the check that nothing needed them.

One person, one visit. A person used to hold a list of attachments and the runtime derived their presence from the list. Now a name is in the room or it is not, and a second visitSession on that name commits nothing and hands back the same visit. A host that shows one person two tabs reconciles the tabs and tells the room one thing. Three runs of this scenario never opened a second attachment for anybody.

No clock writes to the record. A timer used to commit away and returned messages when somebody stopped acting. Across three runs it produced two messages in total. Attention is claimed, not inferred, so the record now carries only what a deliberate act observed: arrived from visitSession, left from leave. Four presence kinds became two, and idleTimeout, the per-visit timer, visit ids, visit.status, visit.acted(), session.visits() and four visit_* events left with them. A presence message now reaches a host on the same delivery event as everything else.

Nothing above it changed. The attention scale still decides who wakes, the catch-up anchor is still the last left on the record, and this run reads the same as the last one.
